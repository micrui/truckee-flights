The jets wait for 7:00.

Early-morning aircraft noise is a real complaint in Truckee: people under the flight path are being woken up. The disputed part is who is doing it. Eight straight mornings of public flight-tracking data show no private jets taking off or landing before 7:00 am, when the airport's voluntary quiet hours end. Several jets started up early and waited on the ground for 7:00. What did fly earlier: a few turboprop planes headed for the Bay Area, one small jet making a 3-minute stop, and an air-ambulance helicopter. A comparable amount of early noise comes from planes that never land here at all.

Reading the data

  • Jet activity starts at 7:00, not before. 34 jet takeoffs and landings across the 8 mornings, by 19 different jets: NetJets and Flexjet charters, a Gulfstream 650, a Global 7500, several Citations, a Challenger. The earliest jet movement each morning: 7:03, 7:12, 7:20, 7:29, 7:37, 7:41, 7:59, 8:11. On Aug 7 a Global 7500 showed up on the tracker at 6:16 and sat on the ground for 97 minutes before taking off at 7:53. On Aug 8 a NetJets Citation started up at 6:41 and took off at 7:41. That is not ignoring the quiet hours. That is watching the clock.
  • The one jet exception is a small Pilatus jet (N281EB, registered through an owner trust). It came over from Reno and landed at 6:46 on Aug 3, sat for 3 minutes, and took off again at 6:49. It shows up 7 times across the week, moving between Truckee, Reno, and Palo Alto. It is the only jet that flew during quiet hours all week. One airplane, not a pattern.
  • Most of the flying before 7:00 is turboprops. 8 of the 12 early movements were turboprops, mostly Pilatus PC-12s: propeller planes with turbine engines, quieter than jets but far from silent. They were flying to and from the Bay Area and beyond (San Carlos, Concord, Modesto, Scottsdale) between 6:13 and 6:44. The voluntary quiet hours cover them the same as jets. On this week's evidence they, not the jets, are the planes flying during quiet hours. Tail numbers are in the table above.
  • Recreational and training flights wait for 7:00 too. 47 flights in the window took off from Truckee and came back to Truckee: one Piper that flew a morning lap on 5 of the 8 days, backcountry Cubs, flight-school Cessnas, and the tow planes that pull gliders up, starting around 8:00. Five of the 47 left before 7:00 all week. Local pleasure and training flying is a large share of morning traffic, and nearly all of it goes after quiet hours end.
  • A lot of what you hear at 6:30 is not this airport. Every morning, two or three aircraft crossed low over the area without ever landing here: small planes passing through at 2,400 to 4,500 ft above the valley floor, turboprops climbing on their way somewhere else, fire and utility helicopters. Add the airline traffic descending into Reno east of the ridge and high-altitude flights overhead, and there is real early-morning engine noise that no Truckee operations log, including this one, will show.
  • The curfew is voluntary. The Airport Noise and Capacity Act of 1990 preempts local mandatory curfews, so KTRK's "Fly Quiet" request (10 pm – 7 am, all aircraft) is backed by hangar-lease incentives rather than enforcement. In this week's data the most expensive aircraft on the field were also the most consistent with it; charter operators such as NetJets and Flexjet carry reputational exposure and appear to schedule around the boundary.
  • Tracking visibility varies by aircraft. Most aircraft here are registered to LLCs and trusts, which is standard liability and estate practice at most asset levels and signals little on its own. Separately, 7 of the 59 aircraft that operated at KTRK this week are enrolled in the FAA's LADD program, which asks commercial tracking sites such as FlightAware not to display them; the 7 include the two most frequent pre-7:00 operators. None used the stronger PIA anonymized-registration program. LADD is legal, free, and grew in adoption after the celebrity jet-tracking accounts of 2022. The practical effect is that the flights most likely to prompt a lookup can be absent from the sites a resident would check first. The raw ADS-B broadcast remains public regardless, which is what this analysis uses.

Method. ADS-B Exchange historical replay data (half-hour heatmap slices, 11:00–15:30 UTC) parsed for all aircraft within 20 nm of KTRK on each of Aug 1–8, 2026; 110–126 aircraft per morning reduced to those within 13 nm below 20,000 ft; full-day position traces then pulled per aircraft and segmented into ground blocks at the field to derive arrival/departure times, origins and destinations. Aircraft type and ownership from the FAA registry via ADS-B Exchange. Reno-bound airliner traffic and high-altitude enroute overflights excluded. FAA privacy-program enrollment (LADD, PIA) read from the same per-aircraft records. Pipeline code, method notes, and the full derived dataset: github.com/micrui/truckee-flights.

Caveats. One summer week; winter patterns may differ. ADS-B only: aircraft without transponders (some gliders, older pistons) are invisible, so piston counts are floors. Departure times at the 8:30 window edge are clipped. A 1-minute landing-takeoff pair (e.g. the Gulfstream 650 on Aug 7) may be a touch-and-go or a ground/air flag artifact. Ownership strings are FAA registry entries; LLCs and trusts obscure beneficial owners.

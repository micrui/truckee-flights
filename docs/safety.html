Accidents and risk at the airport

The documented accident record at Truckee Tahoe Airport, the physical setting that shapes it, the district’s own decisions about it, and what living near a runway end actually means in the state’s own risk framework. Assembled from NTSB records, FAA publications, and district documents before the subject becomes an argument. This page characterizes no flight and no fear; it reports.

The record: sixty years

No one on the ground, ever — and two close calls

In the entire sixty-year record, no person on the ground has been injured or killed by an aircraft accident at Truckee. Both facts that follow are equally part of the record: the July 2021 Challenger came to rest between a Ponderosa Golf Course fairway and a residential street about a mile west-northwest of the field, under the runway 11 final approach; news reporting at the time described it narrowly missing homes and a school bus. The March 2024 TBM came down about 3,200 feet from the runway 11 approach end, near the Olympic Heights neighborhood, striking no structures. The two most recent fatal accidents both fell on the residential side of the airport, and neither touched a house.

Sources: NTSB final report press release (2021 accident) · ASN 318959 · ASN 378178 (2024, preliminary) · CBS Sacramento.

The setting, in the district’s and FAA’s own words

  • The district, to pilots: You will land in a bowl of mountains at 5,901 MSL; Density Altitudes commonly exceed 9,000' in the summer; Winds are squirrely – our 4 wind socks can be 4 different directions; Afternoon winds are typically gusty and windshear is common.
  • The district, on its own accident history: All serious IFR accidents at KTRK in the past 23 years were during circling maneuvers. The record above bears this out: the 2005 Learjet, the 2010 RV-8, and the 2021 Challenger were all lost in low maneuvering to line up with a runway; the 2024 accident, still under investigation, occurred during low maneuvering after a night approach.
  • The FAA’s official airport remarks: mountainous terrain surrounds the airport and runways, expect windshear and downdrafts; summer afternoon density altitudes frequently exceed 9,000 ft; no deicing available; cold-temperature altimetry corrections required.
  • No precision approach exists. The best minimums on the field require weather 582 ft above the runway, with a mandatory climb gradient on the missed approach. Two of the three instrument approaches are not authorized at night, and circling to three of the four runway ends is not authorized at night. The control tower operates roughly 7 am to 6–8 pm seasonally; outside those hours the field is uncontrolled.
  • After the 2021 accident, the NTSB issued a national safety alert on circling approaches (SA-084), counting 10 circling accidents and 17 deaths since 2008.

Sources: district pilot portal · FAA record via AirNav · current FAA approach plates (RNAV 11, RNAV 20, RNAV-A) · NTSB SA-084 announcement.

The district’s decisions

  • The 2015 Master Plan declines to pursue lower approach minimums, and reasons that TRK’s own challenging flight environment (i.e., high altitude, surrounding mountains, and fast-changing weather patterns) … dissuades much of this activity. It weighs whether even a deicing facility might incentivize all-weather activity. The airport’s difficulty is treated, on paper, as a demand-management tool.
  • The control tower (2017, about $860,000 a year of district funds) was established to enhance safety and mitigate community annoyance amid mixed glider, jet, training, and helicopter traffic.
  • After the 2021 accident the district posted the FAA’s safety alert on visual approaches, and in 2024 launched FLY SAFE, paying hangar credits and instructor reimbursements for recurrent pilot training.

Sources: 2015 Airport Master Plan · FY2020 budget · FLY SAFE program.

Risk in context

Two things are true at once. General aviation is safer than it has ever been: the national fatal accident rate has roughly halved since the early 1990s (about 1.8 per 100,000 flight hours then; roughly 1.0 by NTSB measures in 2019–2020, and 0.65–0.68 in 2022–2023 by the AOPA Air Safety Institute’s accounting). And it remains one of the riskiest common activities an American can undertake: personal flying’s per-hour fatality risk sits near motorcycling, well above driving. It is risk of the same kind as the skiing and mountain biking this community practices — voluntary, trainable, unforgiving of complacency — at a magnitude closer to motorcycling than to anything on the hill.

The risk is also sharply stratified: professionally crewed turbine operations approach airline-like safety nationally, while personal flying carries most of the fatal rate. Truckee’s record complicates the stratification honestly: the 2005 Learjet, 2021 Challenger, and 2024 TBM were all turbine aircraft, two of them professionally crewed. The terrain taxes every class of operator.

Sources: NTSB annual GA statistics · AOPA ASI McSpadden Report, 2023 data · AOPA trend summary.

Living near a runway end

The state treats proximity to runway ends as a defined, mapped condition, not a feeling. California’s Airport Land Use Planning Handbook found that near-airport accidents cluster in predictable zones: for runways of Truckee’s size, its six zones capture about 85% of near-airport accident sites, with the highest concentration in the runway protection zones at the runway ends. The Truckee Tahoe Airport Land Use Compatibility Plan (adopted 2016) applies those zones here. It records residential areas to each side of runway centerline within 1.0 mile of runway end northwest of Runway 11 — and that Runway 29 handles 96% of jet takeoffs, meaning most jet departures climb out toward the residential side. Federal and district open space wraps the airport’s east and south; the neighborhoods are northwest.

Inside the mapped zones, the plan limits new residential density (from zero dwellings in the runway protection zones to graduated limits outward) and prohibits new schools, day cares, and hospitals in the inner zones. It has no authority over homes that already exist. California law requires that residential sales within an airport influence area carry an airport-proximity disclosure, so every recent buyer in these neighborhoods received formal notice at closing.

The base rate that closes the loop: nationally, over the decade 2013–2022, NTSB records show 25 accidents in which someone on the ground was killed — two or three a year, against roughly 28 million annual flight hours. The chance that any single flight ends in any single yard is minuscule. The reason the state draws zones anyway is thirty thousand operations a year, every year, concentrated along two extended centerlines. Frequency times geometry, not fear.

Sources: California Airport Land Use Planning Handbook (2011) · Truckee Tahoe Airport Land Use Compatibility Plan (2016) · AOPA (NTSB ground-fatality query).
